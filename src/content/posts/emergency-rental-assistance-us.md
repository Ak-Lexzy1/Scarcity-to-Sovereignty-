You never forget the feeling of finding an eviction notice on your door because nothing prepares you for the panic. The way your stomach drops before you've even finished reading it and the math you start doing in your head that never quite adds up in time.

If you're in that moment right now, I want to tell you two things before anything else. First, this is more common than you think because millions of eviction filings happen every year in the US. Second, real help still exists, even though a lot of what you'll find searching online is outdated.

Here's the honest, current picture, and exactly where to actually look.

## The big program most articles still point you to no longer exists

If you've searched for rental assistance before and found information about a federal Emergency Rental Assistance Program, here's the update that matters: that program is closed. Both phases, known as ERA1 and ERA2, distributed a combined $46.5 billion between 2021 and 2025, but the final performance period ended September 30, 2025, and there is no active nationwide application anymore.

A lot of outdated guides and even some government-adjacent pages still reference it as though it's open. It isn't. I'd rather tell you that plainly upfront than have you waste precious time filling out an application that leads nowhere.

The good news is that help didn't disappear when that program ended. It just moved, and became more local, more scattered, and honestly, a lot less visible unless you know exactly where to look.

## Start here: call 211

Before anything else, call **211**, or visit **211.org**. This is a free hotline operated by United Way, covering all 50 states, Washington DC, and Puerto Rico. It connects you directly to whatever rental assistance is currently funded in your specific county, since availability shifts constantly at the local level in a way no single website can keep up with in real time. This is genuinely the fastest, most reliable first step, and it costs nothing.

## Organizations still actively helping right now

**The Salvation Army**
Confirmed active nationwide, with over 7,000 locations across the country. Unlike the old federal program, this one is funded through the Salvation Army's own operating budget, not government grants, which is exactly why it kept running when ERA wound down. Typical assistance runs $200 to $600 in gap funding, paid directly to your landlord rather than to you.

Apply at [salvationarmyusa.org](https://www.salvationarmyusa.org)

**Catholic Charities**
Faith-based, but assistance is generally available regardless of your religious affiliation. This organization has provided rental and emergency assistance continuously throughout the wind-down of federal programs, and remains one of the more consistently funded options nationally.

Apply at [catholiccharitiesusa.org](https://www.catholiccharitiesusa.org)

**HUD's ongoing housing programs**
These aren't emergency one-time grants, they're permanent federal housing support that never depended on the ERA program in the first place:

- **Housing Choice Voucher Program (Section 8)** — ongoing rental subsidy for eligible low-income households
- **HUD-VASH** — specifically for veterans experiencing housing instability
- **Section 811** — for non-elderly disabled adults, with HUD recently announcing $158 million in new funding specifically for this program

Learn more at [hud.gov](https://www.hud.gov)

**Community Development Block Grants (CDBG)**
Federal money that still flows to local cities and counties, administered at the local level rather than through a national application. The most effective approach here is searching "[your city or county] emergency rental assistance" directly, since these programs rarely show up in general national searches.

## The genuinely underused resource: Right to Counsel

This is the part of this post I most want you to walk away with, because the data behind it is honestly staggering, and almost nobody knows it exists.

In eviction court, tenants have legal representation only **3% of the time**. Landlords are represented **80% of the time**, in those exact same cases. That's not a small imbalance. In most eviction hearings across the country, one side has a lawyer and the other side doesn't, before the hearing even starts.

Right to Counsel programs exist specifically to close that gap, a legal guarantee of a free lawyer for eligible tenants facing eviction. Where they've been implemented, the results are dramatic and well documented:

- **Connecticut:** 71% of tenants who used the program avoided being forced to move, and 76% avoided the eviction ever appearing on their record at all
- **Toledo, Ohio:** 88% of represented tenants avoided eviction entirely
- **Denver:** more than half of represented tenants either avoided an eviction judgment or had their case dismissed outright

**Here's the honest limitation:** these programs currently exist in only a limited number of states and roughly 15 cities nationwide. It's not universal yet. But if you're in one of those areas, this is arguably the single highest-leverage resource on this entire list, and it's almost never the first thing people think to search for.

Check whether your area has one through the National Coalition for a Civil Right to Counsel at [civilrighttocounsel.org](https://civilrighttocounsel.org), or search NLIHC's State and Local Tenant Protections Database at [nlihc.org/tenant-protections](https://nlihc.org/tenant-protections).

## What I'm intentionally leaving off this list

You'll see Modest Needs referenced on some other rental assistance guides as an active resource. I'm excluding it, and I want to be direct about why: its founder was convicted of embezzling donor funds, and multiple watchdog organizations have since pulled their recommendation of the organization entirely. The fact that other sites still list it as current is exactly why I'd rather double-check every resource before sending you somewhere, instead of just recycling whatever's already circulating online.

## If you're already facing an eviction notice, act now, not later

An eviction notice is a legal document with a real deadline attached, and every day matters. Don't wait until the court date to start looking for help. Call 211 first, apply to the Salvation Army or Catholic Charities in parallel rather than waiting on one response before trying the next, and check for a Right to Counsel program in your area immediately if you're already facing a court filing. Most agencies move faster when you've already started the process, not when you show up at the last possible moment.
