## What Credit Counseling Is

Simply put, credit counseling is a conversation with a trained counselor about your money. They look at your income, bills, and debts, then help you build a budget and a plan.

That's it. It's not a loan. It's not a magic eraser. It's not a company that pays your balances off for you.

A good session can end in one of a few places. You might leave with a tighter budget and a plan you manage yourself. You might be offered a debt management plan. Or the counselor might point you toward other options, including bankruptcy counseling, if your situation calls for it.

The key word is "options." A reputable counselor lays out choices, while a bad one pushes a single product and tries to make you see it as the best available one.

## Who the NFCC Is and Why It Should Matter to You

The National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC) is a nonprofit network of member agencies across the country. It's not one big counselor you call. It's a group that sets standards and connects you with agencies that meet them.

The NFCC says it has been around since 1951. Its website also says its member network has helped over 35 million people since 2006. That's the organization's own figure, so I'll say you should treat it as a claim rather than an audit.

Here's why membership means something. NFCC publishes quality standards for its members. In plain English, they require:

- **Accreditation:** Agencies must hold recognized accreditation to keep their membership.
- **Trained counselors:** Counselors must be certified, and agencies run background checks before they start.
- **No sales commissions:** Counselors can't be paid extra for signing people up for DMPs.
- **Nonprofit rules:** Agencies must follow 501(c)(3) rules, file public tax forms, and get an annual independent audit.
- **Protected client money:** Funds you send for a plan must sit in a separate, insured account.
- **Limits on fees:** Fees should be kept as low as possible, and they can't be collected in advance.
- **Time limits:** Plans should generally be repayable within 60 months.

That's a real checklist, and it's much stronger than the standard for a random company with "credit" in its name.

One honest heads-up I will add is that membership improves your odds but doesn't replace your own homework. Also, fees and policies still differ by agency.

**Official website:** [https://www.nfcc.org](https://www.nfcc.org)
**Agency Finder:** [https://www.nfcc.org/agency-finder/](https://www.nfcc.org/agency-finder/)
**Phone:** 800-388-2227

The NFCC says it offers service in English and Spanish, and says there are no upfront fees and no obligation to buy anything when you reach out.

## What Happens in the Free First Session

The NFCC says the first session is always free. Agencies I checked describe it as confidential, and it can happen by phone, online, or in person. One agency says about 30 minutes, but I'll advise you plan on 30 to 60 minutes. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has described a reputable first session as typically about an hour.

Here's what usually happens:

1. **The counselor asks about your goals:** What are you trying to fix? What's scaring you most?
2. **You go over income and expenses:** Every dollar in, every dollar out.
3. **You review your debts:** Balances, interest rates, minimum payments.
4. **They may check your credit report:** One agency says it uses a soft inquiry, which doesn't lower your score. Ask before you assume.
5. **You leave with a plan:** It might include a budget, tips, and a follow-up appointment.

**What to bring (it helps, but it's optional):**

- A list of your debts with balances and interest rates
- Recent pay stubs or income proof
- Your main monthly bills
- A rough idea of what you spend on food, gas, and everyday life

Also, you're not expected to arrive organized. Showing up scared and messy is completely normal, so you really don't have to be uptight even before you step out of your house.

## What a Debt Management Plan (DMP) Is

A DMP is one possible result of your session. It's not automatic, and you don't have to say yes.

Here's how it works: You make one monthly payment to the agency. The agency then pays your unsecured creditors, like credit cards, for you on a schedule. The agency may also ask your creditors to lower your interest rates or waive some fees.

Notice the word "ask." Creditors decide whether to agree because nothing is guaranteed. I need to tell you this plainly so you don't get your hopes up.

The FTC advises you to confirm any rate reduction directly with each creditor and to ask how long before it starts. It also says a DMP can take 48 months or more.

### What a DMP costs

This is where the "free" claim stops. The first session is free, but the plan is not.

At the agencies I checked, here's roughly what DMP fees looked like:

| Fee | Typical average | Highest cap I saw |
|---|---|---|
| One-time setup fee | About $35 to $52 | Up to $100 |
| Monthly fee | About $26 to $34 | Up to $75 |

These are averages published by the agencies and state caps vary, so your numbers may differ. Some agencies reduce fees for hardship. One I checked said its fees can be reduced but not waived. So ask each agency about its policy directly.

Federal guidance also protects you here. The FTC says a company can't collect a DMP fee until you've entered the plan and made at least one payment through it.

### A worked example (my own math)

Say you owe $12,000 across credit cards averaging 24% APR. You pay $360 a month on your own.

- **On your own:** About 55 months, with roughly $7,970 in interest.
- **On a DMP:** Suppose the agency gets your rates down to about 8%. You pay $360 a month, with a $34 monthly fee inside it and a $40 setup fee. That finishes in about 42 months, with roughly $1,810 in interest plus about $1,500 in fees.

Total cost on your own: about $7,970 in interest. Total cost on the plan: about $3,300. That's a difference of around $4,700, and the plan ends about 13 months sooner.

Now for the honest part, so you don't get overly excited without full understanding. This is an illustration. It only works if creditors actually agree to those lower rates. Two agencies publish their own averages, with one reporting accounts dropping from about 28% to under 8% in 2025. Those are the agencies' self-reported numbers, not guarantees or independent findings.

## What Debts a DMP Covers (and What It Doesn't)

**Usually covered:** Credit cards and some unsecured personal loans. Creditors still have to participate.

**Usually not covered:**

- Mortgages
- Auto loans
- Other secured debts (debts backed by property)
- Federal student loans, which have their own repayment and relief options through [https://studentaid.gov](https://studentaid.gov)

If your biggest problem is federal student loans, a DMP likely isn't your tool. But a counselor can still help you budget around them.

## Will a DMP Hurt Your Credit?

My short answer is that it can dip first, then recover.

A DMP itself is not a scoring factor. But enrolled credit cards are often closed. Closing accounts changes your credit utilization, and that can cause a temporary dip.

The upside is on-time payments. Steady payments and shrinking balances usually help your score recover over time. One agency says "within a few months" to a year, but that varies by person.

Two warnings:

- **Missing payments can cancel the plan:** This can make your old interest rates come back.
- **Negative history stays:** The FTC notes that accurate negative information can remain on your report for up to seven years.

You may also need to avoid using new credit while you're enrolled. That's a feature, not just a rule. The plan only works if the balances stop growing.

## Credit Counseling vs Debt Settlement vs Bankruptcy

I've noticed people confuse these constantly. They are very different, and the simplest explanation is the table I drafted below.

| | Credit Counseling / DMP | Debt Settlement | Bankruptcy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who runs it | Usually nonprofit agencies | Usually for-profit companies | Court process, often with an attorney |
| What you pay | Free first session, then small plan fees | Often a percentage of the debt | Court and attorney costs |
| Main idea | Lower rates, one payment, repay in full | Pay less than you owe | Legal discharge or repayment plan |
| Big risk | Creditors may not agree, plan can fail | Missed payments, fees, damaged credit | Long credit impact |

A few notes on this table:

- **Settlement companies** typically tell you to stop paying creditors while they negotiate. That can wreck your credit and trigger collections. They're not the same as nonprofit counseling, even when the ads sound alike.
- **Bankruptcy counseling is a different thing:** If you file for bankruptcy, you must complete credit counseling first through an agency approved by the US Trustee Program. You can complete it up to 180 days before filing. The US Trustee's Office has said a "reasonable" fee might run from free to about $50. A full fee waiver applies if your income is at or below 150% of the poverty level for your family size.
- **Official list of approved bankruptcy counseling agencies:** [https://www.justice.gov/ust/credit-counseling-debtor-education-information](https://www.justice.gov/ust/credit-counseling-debtor-education-information)

Not every debt problem needs a DMP. Sometimes the answer is a budget. Sometimes it's bankruptcy. A good counselor will tell you which.

## 8 Questions to Ask Any Credit Counseling Agency

The FTC's guidance suggests asking these before you commit. I've put them in my own words.

1. What services do you offer besides a DMP, like budgeting help or classes?
2. What are your setup and monthly fees, and can I get them in writing?
3. Will you help me avoid this problem again, not just fix today's?
4. Will I get a written contract before I pay anything?
5. Are you licensed in my state?
6. What training do your counselors have?
7. How do you protect my personal and financial information?
8. How are your counselors paid? (If they earn more when you sign up, walk away.)

Take notes. A good agency won't mind.

## Red Flags: How to Spot a Debt Relief Scam

The FTC lists warning signs for bad credit counselors. Watch for these:

- They won't send information until you hand over your financial details.
- They charge for basic information or materials.
- They only offer a DMP before looking at your whole situation.
- They refuse to help because you can't afford their fees.
- They promise to remove accurate negative items from your credit report. Nobody can legally do that.
- They guarantee a result.

### A history lesson worth knowing

The FTC and several states sued a company called AmeriDebt around 2003. They alleged it falsely presented itself as a nonprofit while generating money for for-profit affiliates. They also alleged it hid upfront fees and pushed nearly everyone into DMPs. The company disputed those claims.

That's a historical case, not a verdict about today's agencies. But it shows why "nonprofit" on a website means nothing on its own. The FTC itself says nonprofit status doesn't guarantee services are free, affordable, or legitimate.

**Verify before you pay:**

- Check your state attorney general's office and local consumer protection agency for complaints.
- Confirm the agency is an NFCC member through [the Agency Finder](https://www.nfcc.org/agency-finder/).
- Know that a lack of complaints isn't proof of legitimacy.

## Who Credit Counseling Is Not Right For

Being honest here saves you time, and that's what I'm doing:

- **If your income can't cover even a reduced plan payment:** The FTC says not to enroll in a DMP you can't afford. A counselor can still help you find budget room first.
- **If most of your debt is secured or federal student loans:** A DMP won't cover those.
- **If your debt is mostly past the point of repayment:** Bankruptcy counseling and a legal consultation may be the better route.

## How You Can Get Started Today

1. **Write down your debts:** Names, balances, rates, minimums. Even a rough list works.
2. **Visit the NFCC Agency Finder** at [https://www.nfcc.org/agency-finder/](https://www.nfcc.org/agency-finder/) or call 800-388-2227.
3. **Book the free session:** Phone and online are common.
4. **Ask your eight questions:** Get fees in writing. (You can scroll back up to read them again.)
5. **Don't sign anything the same day:** Take it home and read it.
6. **Keep paying your bills:** The FTC advises paying until creditors approve any plan, then calling your creditors the first of each month to confirm the agency paid on time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is credit counseling really free?

The first session is free through NFCC. A DMP has fees, so ask for the exact setup and monthly amounts before you enroll.

### What is the NFCC?

It's a nonprofit network of member agencies that follow published quality standards. You can find one through the Agency Finder at nfcc.org.

### Does credit counseling hurt your credit score?

Counseling itself doesn't. A DMP can cause a short dip if accounts close, then helps over time with on-time payments.

### How long does a debt management plan take?

Plans are generally designed to finish within 60 months. The FTC says a DMP can take 48 months or more, and some agencies report about three to four years on average.

### What debts can a DMP include?

Mostly unsecured debt like credit cards. Mortgages, car loans, and federal student loans usually are not included.

### Is debt settlement the same as credit counseling?

No. Settlement usually involves for-profit companies and often means you stop paying creditors. Credit counseling is typically nonprofit and focuses on budgeting and repayment in full.

### Can I do this without a counselor?

Yes. You can call creditors yourself and ask for hardship options. A counselor adds structure and sometimes lower rates, but it's not mandatory.

## The Bottom Line I'll Leave You With

Real help exists, and the first step costs nothing. But it only works if you pick a legitimate agency and know what you're agreeing to.

Start with the free session. Ask your questions. Never pay a fee before you understand it. And if anything sounds too good to be true, it is.
