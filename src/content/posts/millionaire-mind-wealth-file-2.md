My last post was only supposed to be Wealth File #1 from Secrets of the Millionaire Mind and as I kept reading, I didn't get far before I had to stop again because I had this nudge: "Why stop at one file? Why not do all 17 files from the book?"

That nudge birth this post. And truly, Wealth File #2 hits differently, just as Wealth File #1 did.

File #2 opens with _"Rich people play the money game to win."_ Poor people, he argues, play mostly to avoid losing. Just like Wealth File #1 gave me a breakthrough, Wealth File #2 got me thinking differently about how the rich actually play the money game.

I share my thoughts below.

## What Wealth File #2 actually says

Most of us approach money defensively. The goal is to avoid debt, pay the bills, keep a little in savings, and make sure nothing goes terribly wrong. That's understandable, especially when life feels uncertain.

Eker's argument is that people with a millionaire mindset treat money differently. They see it as a game they intend to win, not just survive. What they want is financial freedom: real assets, and the ability to make choices without money steering every one of them.

One honest note before we go further. This is Eker's framework, not settled science. Treat it as a lens to test against your own life, not a verdict on anyone.

## Survival mode isn't a character flaw

Statements like "rich people think this, poor people think that" can land like a judgment. They shouldn't. When rent is due and your cushion is thin, playing defense is the smart move. Nobody should feel bad for protecting what they have.

The real question is whether defense is the only game you ever play. Passing through survival mode on your way somewhere is one thing. Living there permanently is another. If it feels more like a loop than a season, [Why Am I Always Broke? (The Survival Loop Keeping You Paycheck to Paycheck)](/blog/escape-being-broke) digs into how that cycle holds on.

## What winning with money looks like (the restaurant test)

Here's a picture that made it concrete for me. Imagine walking into a high-end restaurant and ordering without scanning the prices first. Cost isn't part of the decision. That small bit of leeway and liberty is what money looks like when it stops running the show.

Not everyone wants that exact life, and that's fine. The point is that winning is a picture you get to paint yourself. It doesn't mean being reckless. It means deciding what a good outcome looks like for you, because that answer is different for everyone.

## "I want to be comfortable" is not a financial goal

If you never define winning, you can earn money for decades and never feel it's enough. The finish line keeps moving because it was never drawn.

"I want to be comfortable" is almost impossible to measure. You can't tell when you've arrived. Compare that with goals like these:

- I want to build a net worth of $X.
- I want to retire at age X.
- I want to own assets that produce income.
- I want multiple sources of income.
- I want to fund my lifestyle without depending entirely on active work.

Every one of those gives you something to build toward. You can track progress against them, adjust them, and celebrate when you hit them. You can't do any of that with "comfortable."

## Find your magic number

This is why I strongly advocate that everyone have a magic number. It gives you a goal to drive at and a figure to aim for.

Here's a simple illustration, not a recommendation. Say you want $4,000 a month coming in without relying on a paycheck. That's $48,000 a year. Your magic number is the combined assets and income streams it would take to produce that reliably. A licensed financial planner can help you pressure-test the math.

Your number won't be perfect on day one. Write it down anyway, and revisit it once or thrice a year. A rough target beats no target.

## The hidden cost of playing not to lose

This is where Wealth File #2 challenges small financial thinking. If your whole strategy revolves around avoiding losses, you may never take the calculated risks that building wealth usually involves. That could mean investing, starting something of your own, learning a high-income skill, or finally asking for the raise you keep rehearsing.

Think of a team protecting a lead in the final minutes. Playing safe works, until it doesn't. A strategy that only plays defense has no way to score.

Now the honest part. Risk means you can lose, which is why the word "calculated" matters. Cover your basics first: high-interest debt, a small emergency fund, and money you won't need anytime soon. I walk through those checks in [How to Start Investing With Little Money (From Zero to First $100)](/blog/investing-from-zero). Calculated risk is the opposite of reckless.

## 3 practical actions to start playing to win

Here are the three actions I gave myself the first time I read the book, three years ago.

1. **Write down your definition of winning financially:** one or two sentences is enough. If you're stuck, the exercise in [How to Make a Budget for Beginners: The One Question Before You Start](/blog/before-you-budget) is a good place to begin.
2. **Put numbers around it:** attach figures to your desired income, net worth, investments, and freedom. For freedom, try counting how many months you could stop working.
3. **Ask yourself two questions:** What has to be true for me to get there? And what do I have to do to make it true? Turn your answers into three next steps with dates beside them.
