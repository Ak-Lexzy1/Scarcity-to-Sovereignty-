Most people assume the hardest part of leaving is the leaving. For many survivors, the harder stretch comes afterward: a bank account that was never really yours, a credit report you're afraid to open, and a rent payment with nothing behind it. Money is often what keeps someone in an unsafe home longer than they wanted to stay, and what makes starting over feel impossible once they're out.

That isn't a failing on your part, and it isn't rare. Real help exists for the money side of this, and some of it is legally yours by right. Some of it also has limits nobody mentions up front, and you deserve to hear those before you count on anything. This guide covers all of it, and it applies to survivors of any gender.

## What financial abuse actually looks like

Financial abuse is when a partner controls how you earn, spend, save, or borrow. It can look like an "allowance," a seized paycheck, being forbidden to work, or credit cards and loans opened in your name without your real consent.

It is also close to universal. Kim Pentico, who directs the economic justice program at the National Network to End Domestic Violence (NNEDV), says up to 99% of survivors face some form of it. Researchers who surveyed survivors already receiving services have landed in a similar 94% to 99% range. Purple Purse, an Allstate Foundation campaign, has reported that 78% of people don't realize financial abuse is real, which helps explain why so many survivors never get to name what happened to them.

The part that follows you out the door is called coerced debt: debt created in your name through pressure, threats, or fraud. The Center for Survivor Agency and Justice reports that about half of survivors have it. Among them, about half report balances up to $20,000, 23% report more, and roughly a quarter aren't even sure how much they owe. Some 46% say an abusive partner damaged their credit, which can then block them from renting an apartment, turning on utilities, or getting hired.

## Start with one safe conversation: the National Domestic Violence Hotline

The Hotline has been running since 1996, and here is how to reach it:

- **Call:** 1-800-799-7233 (SAFE)
- **Text:** START to 88788
- **Chat:** [thehotline.org](https://www.thehotline.org), which you can use silently if you can't speak freely
- **TTY:** 1-800-787-3224

It's free, confidential, open around the clock, and has interpreters available in more than 200 languages. Advocates listen, help you build a safety plan, explain your options, and connect you with local programs.

**What it can't do:** the Hotline does not give out money, hotel vouchers, or transportation vouchers. It refers you to local programs that might. Think of it as the map, not the cash. Wait times can run long when demand is high, so if a call sits in the queue, try chat or text. It isn't an emergency service either, so in immediate danger, call 911.

**Two more national lines:** the StrongHearts Native Helpline (844-762-8483) serves Native Americans and Alaska Natives, and the National Teen Dating Abuse Helpline is 866-331-9474.

**For local help**, every state and territory has a domestic violence coalition. NNEDV represents all 56 of them, and you can find yours at [nnedv.org](https://nnedv.org). Your coalition can point you to shelters, legal help, and programs that actually have money right now.

**One practical rule:** before you close a joint account, change your mailing address, or file anything with a creditor, ask an advocate how it fits your safety plan. Some moves that make perfect financial sense can alert an abuser.

## Crime victim compensation: money you may be owed

**Every state runs a compensation program:** for victims of violent crime, domestic violence included. These programs generally reimburse costs like medical care, counseling, and lost wages after other sources, such as health insurance, have paid. Some states cover more.

**Amounts differ a lot:** in the state programs I checked, Missouri caps awards at $25,000, North Carolina at $45,000, and Ohio at $50,000. Ohio's program can even pay for an attorney to help you get a protective order in some cases.

**Rules differ too:** most states want a police report, but many make exceptions for domestic violence. Rhode Island, for example, accepts a no-contact order in its place. Filing windows run from about one year in many states to seven in California, and some states extend them for good cause. So don't assume you've missed your chance. Check your state's rules.

To apply, find your state's program through the directory at [nacvcb.org](https://nacvcb.org). Most states let you apply online, by paper form, or with an advocate's help.

**The honest limits:** many programs reimburse after the fact, so keep every bill and receipt. Claims can be denied for missed deadlines or unmet requirements. And the funding behind these programs is under strain, which I cover below.

## Your housing rights: strong, but not universal

If you live in federally assisted housing, the Violence Against Women Act (VAWA) protects you whatever your gender. That includes public housing, Housing Choice vouchers, project-based rental assistance, and other HUD-assisted programs. In those homes, a provider can't deny you housing, evict you, or end your assistance because of abuse against you. That includes cases where the abuse left you with an eviction record or bad credit.

You can request an emergency transfer for safety, and for voucher holders that can mean taking your voucher with you. A provider can also split a lease to remove the abuser while you stay. Providers must give you a written notice of these rights (Form HUD-5380) at key moments, including when you're admitted and when you face an eviction or termination, along with a self-certification form (HUD-5382). If a housing agency ignores your rights, you can file a complaint with HUD. Details are at [hud.gov/vawa](https://www.hud.gov/vawa).

**The gap:** VAWA's housing protections don't reach private housing with no federal money behind it. Many states have their own laws letting survivors end a lease early with documentation, so look up your state's rules before you break a lease on your own.

Also ask your advocate about an Address Confidentiality Program. Many states run one, giving you a substitute mailing address with mail forwarding so your real address stays off public records.

## Repairing your credit and fighting coerced debt

Take these in order:

1. **Choose a safe mailing address first:** Legal aid groups advise doing this before any financial paperwork, since statements and letters can land in the wrong hands.
2. **Pull your credit reports:** from Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion through the official site, [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com). If you can, review them with a financial counselor or economic empowerment specialist beside you.
3. **File an identity theft report with the FTC:** at [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov). Attorneys who work with survivors often call this the safest first step, because it generally doesn't trigger a direct investigation of your abuser.
4. **If it's safe, also file a police report:** Bring the FTC report, your credit report, and any bills tied to the debt.
5. **Send those documents to each bureau:** and ask for an identity theft block, which keeps fraudulent accounts off your report. Dispute the debts with the creditors in writing too.
6. **Consider a security freeze:** this stops anyone from opening new accounts in your name.

Some states go further. Texas treats debt obtained through force, threat, or fraud as identity theft for debts incurred on or after September 1, 2019. California has a court process for blocking collection of coerced debt, and Maine offers related protections.

**The honest limit:** a 2025 report from the National Consumer Law Center found that credit bureaus have sometimes refused to block coerced debt even when survivors submitted an FTC report. Expect pushback, keep copies of everything, and bring in a legal aid or consumer attorney if a bureau stonewalls you. A refusal doesn't strip you of your rights. You can still dispute inaccurate information under the Fair Credit Reporting Act.

## What the research actually says about the holidays

You'll often read that domestic violence spikes at the holidays. The evidence doesn't support that. The National Resource Center on Domestic Violence found no reliable national study linking the two, and the Hotline's own call data points the other way: in 2015 it averaged 837 calls a day but only about 450 on Christmas Day.

That doesn't make the season easy, and it doesn't mean abuse pauses. Advocates say survivors may be less able to call, or may feel pressure to keep the family together, then reach out after the new year. The season also adds real strain: custody handoffs, restraining-order logistics, money stress, and being around relatives. So treat it as a window to prepare, not a reason to panic. Save the Hotline number somewhere safe, ask an advocate which documents are worth gathering, and learn your options before January, when many people reach out.

## The honest news about funding

Most local domestic violence programs rely on the federal Crime Victims Fund, and it's in trouble. The fund is built from federal fines and penalties, not tax dollars. Annual deposits fell from about $2.9 billion a year (2010 to 2017) to about $1.3 billion (2018 to 2025). A roughly 40% cut to victim assistance grants between 2022 and 2024 forced providers to shorten hours, lay off staff, and in some places close. NNEDV reported that on a single day in 2023, programs served nearly 80,000 survivors but had to turn away more than 13,000 requests for lack of funding.

In January 2026, the House passed the Crime Victims Fund Stabilization Act, which would redirect certain False Claims Act money into the fund through fiscal 2029. As of this review, NNEDV is still urging the Senate to act, and I can't tell you how it will end.

What that means for you: the program near you may be stretched, have a waitlist, or keep limited hours. Call early, try more than one, and don't read a busy signal as proof that no help exists. Your state coalition can tell you what's actually running.

## Your first week, in order

1. Get on a safer device.
2. Call, text, or chat with the Hotline or your state coalition, and ask for a local advocate.
3. Set up a safe mailing address.
4. Ask the advocate about crime victim compensation and your state's deadline.
5. If you live in subsidized housing, ask about VAWA protections.
6. When it's safe, start the credit steps with an advocate or counselor.

You don't have to finish this in a week, and you don't have to do it alone. None of it undoes what happened to you. What it gives you is leverage, a paper trail, and some breathing room.
