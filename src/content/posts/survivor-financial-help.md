Most people assume the hardest part of leaving is the leaving. For many survivors, the harder stretch comes afterward: a bank account that was never really yours, a credit report you're afraid to open, and a rent payment with nothing behind it. Money is often what keeps someone in an unsafe home longer than they wanted to stay, and what makes starting over feel impossible once they're out.

That isn't a failing on your part, and it isn't rare. Real help exists for the money side of this, and some of it is legally yours by right. Some of it also has limits nobody mentions up front, and you deserve to hear those before you count on anything. This guide covers all of it, and it applies to survivors of any gender.

## What financial abuse actually looks like

Financial abuse is when a partner controls how you earn, spend, save, or borrow. It can look like an "allowance," a seized paycheck, being forbidden to work, or credit cards and loans opened in your name without your real consent.

It is also close to universal. Kim Pentico, who directs the economic justice program at the National Network to End Domestic Violence (NNEDV), says up to 99% of survivors face some form of it. Researchers who surveyed survivors already receiving services have landed in a similar 94% to 99% range. Purple Purse, an Allstate Foundation campaign, has reported that 78% of people don't realize financial abuse is real, which helps explain why so many survivors never get to name what happened to them.

The part that follows you out the door is called coerced debt: debt created in your name through pressure, threats, or fraud. The Center for Survivor Agency and Justice reports that about half of survivors have it.
