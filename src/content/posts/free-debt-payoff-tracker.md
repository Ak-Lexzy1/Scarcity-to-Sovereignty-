## Let's Look at Real Numbers

I don't like vague advice about debt. So here's a worked example I ran myself.

Say you have a $5,000 balance on a card charging 22% APR. For context, the Federal Reserve's latest data shows the average rate on accounts carrying interest sits around 22.4%, so this isn't an extreme case.

Here's what different monthly payments do:

| Monthly Payment | Time to Pay Off | Total Interest Paid |
|---|---|---|
| $125 | About 6 years (73 months) | About $4,090 |
| $200 | About 2 years 10 months (34 months) | About $1,750 |
| $300 | About 1 year 8 months (20 months) | About $1,020 |

Read that again. At $125 a month, you'd pay almost as much in interest as you originally borrowed.

Now look at the jump from $200 to $300. That extra $100 a month gets you out **14 months sooner** and saves roughly **$730**.

These are estimates. They assume a fixed rate and no new purchases. But the lesson is real. Small increases in payment make a big difference, because interest is calculated on a shrinking balance.

That's exactly what the single card tracker helps you see.

## Debt Snowball vs Debt Avalanche: Which One Actually Works?

Both methods start the same way. Pay the minimum on every debt. Then put all your extra money toward one target.

The difference is which debt you pick.

- **Snowball:** target the smallest balance first, no matter the interest rate.
- **Avalanche:** target the highest interest rate first, no matter the balance.

Here's a real comparison. Imagine three debts and $450 a month to put toward them:

- Card A: $4,800 at 29% APR (minimum $120)
- Card B: $1,500 at 18% APR (minimum $45)
- Car loan: $6,000 at 9% APR (minimum $140)

I ran both methods. Both finish in about 35 months. But the avalanche costs about $3,100 in interest, while the snowball costs about $3,410.

So the avalanche saves roughly **$314** here.

That's real money, but it's not life-changing. And that's the honest point most articles skip. When your interest rates are close together, the gap shrinks, but when they're far apart (like a 29% card next to a 9% loan), the gap grows.

Now here's the other side. Researchers Gal and McShane studied thousands of people in debt settlement programs. They found the number of accounts someone **closed** was a stronger predictor of finishing than the dollar amount paid. In plain English: small wins keep people going.

So which should you pick?

- Pick the **avalanche** if you're disciplined and one card is charging a brutal rate.
- Pick the **snowball** if you've quit before, or if motivation is your real problem.
- Pick the **one you'll actually finish.** A perfect plan you abandon loses to a decent plan you complete.

Neither one is cheating. Both beat doing nothing.

## How to Start Today (Even If You're Overwhelmed)

You don't need a perfect plan. You need a first step small enough to take tonight.

1. **List every debt:** Name, balance, interest rate, minimum payment. Log in and look at each one, even if it hurts.
2. **Find your extra money:** Even $25 a month counts. If you can't find it, run a no-spend week and see what appears.
3. **Pick your method:** Snowball or avalanche. Decide in five minutes, not five weeks.
4. **Print the tracker and fill it in:** Do that today, before you talk yourself out of it.
5. **Update it every payment:** Two minutes. Color a block. Move on.

And please don't skip the emotional part. Debt carries shame, and shame makes people avoid their own statements. You're not bad with money. You were handed a system designed to profit from your balance. Looking at the number is the bravest part.

## A Few Honest Heads-Ups

- **Keep paying every minimum:** Missing one can trigger late fees and a higher rate, which undoes your progress.
- **Build a small buffer:** Even $500 to $1,000 keeps an emergency from landing back on the card.
- **Stop adding to the balance:** A tracker can't save you if new charges keep refilling the card.
- **Struggling badly?** Free nonprofit credit counseling exists. Look for an agency affiliated with the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC) rather than a company promising to "erase" your debt.
