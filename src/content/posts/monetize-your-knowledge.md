I mentioned in one of my earlier posts that I'm a natural-born writer, but I didn't realize it was a talent I could actually monetize until some of my friends, who weren't nearly as good at it as I was, went through writing training and started making real money offering it as a service.

By the time I finally saw the opportunity and decided to start monetizing my own knowledge, through ghostwriting and eventually teaching people how to write, a monster had already crept into my thoughts. I never saw it coming. That feeling made me feel like a fraud, despite the talent I clearly had.

It's a long story, and it took me over a year of deliberate, torturous work to get that monstrous feeling out of my head. I might share the full details in another post sometime, but for now, I'll leave you with a well-known example you've probably heard of.

Maya Angelou wrote eleven books. She won a Grammy. She was nominated for a Pulitzer Prize. She read a poem at a presidential inauguration in front of the entire country. And for years, she carried a secret fear that had nothing to do with her talent and everything to do with her mind.

She once admitted that after each book she published, the same thought crept in: that people were eventually going to find out she'd been "running a game" on everyone the whole time.

Read that again. A woman with eleven published books, international acclaim, and a body of work still studied in classrooms today, quietly convinced somewhere inside herself that she didn't really belong there. If imposter syndrome can follow someone with that level of proof behind her, it can absolutely be lying to you too, just as it did to me.

The imposter syndrome is that voice you know. That familiar voice in your head saying, "I'm not an authority yet. Who am I to teach others what I know?"

Just like me, you won't know it for what it truly is at first, because it would seem like it's just insecurity talking. Trust me, it's not reality, and it's something more deadly. It's a shithole, because I was there for over a year.

But here is the ugly truth and the emotion underneath it. Follow me.

When you finally push yourself to monetize your knowledge and create your course, and then you see people online mocking course creators, it suddenly feels personal. Shame creeps in, like they're talking directly to you.

That feeling is normal, especially if you're not seeing results yet. It doesn't mean they're right. It's because you're just starting out, and the positive results that will come are going to be the drive that validates your guts.

This is the transition period imposter syndrome pounces on, making sure you doubt yourself or stop midway, before you can connect your current starting-out phase to the results phase still ahead of you.

## There are two reasons you need to monetize your knowledge

**Reason one: Passive income.**
Monetizing your knowledge is like owning an intellectual asset. You may not own a big physical asset like a house or car yet, but your knowledge can pay you monthly, the same way rent does for a landlord. It's genuinely one of the easiest assets you can build at your current level, whatever that level is.

Ironically, the first set of writing gigs I got, starting out, paid my rent for several months, with decent leftovers each month for other important needs.

**Reason two: You already have the results, even if they feel small.**
If you can make $8,000 a month with relative ease, there's someone out there struggling to make $1,500 or $3,000. To that person, you're already the expert, and you only need to know 1% more than your customers, clients, or leads to qualify to teach them.

And that brings me to the perspective shift.

The key here is simple. Teach your old self. Who were you two or three years ago? Who are you today? There are people right now standing exactly where you used to stand, and they need the roadmap you already walked.

I often used myself as an example during my classes back then. I would tell my students that I was once in their shoes, and I'd always see myself in them when teaching. This made me understand them so well, and I taught in a way they found very profound. They usually gave feedback saying it was as if I'd been reading their mind about what they really wanted to learn.

Next comes the mindset flip.

As you're reading this and getting inspired, don't let shame and imposter syndrome stop you from monetizing your knowledge and building a real asset. Teach honestly. Sell genuinely. Profit ethically.

You've got to sell to your old self, and you'll never feel like a fraud again. That was what a mentor told me when I discussed my imposter syndrome situation with them. Like I said earlier, this method helped me, and it even improved the quality of my classes and services.

During the time I was starting out, a friend made a subtle joke in passing about my writing gig, which made me feel uncomfortable. The truth I later discovered from that experience was that most people don't mock those who monetize their knowledge because they think they're scammers. They mock them because they're insecure about their own lack of progress. And that's just the point.

You don't need to fake success to teach what you know. You just need to teach what you've actually learned, with honesty. If your brain can make money, your brain can build an asset. So stop letting shame and imposter syndrome hold you back.

Teach your old self. Build your asset. Own your results.
