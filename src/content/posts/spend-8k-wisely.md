I'd just finished a five-hour stretch on a book project and took a break to doomscroll, the way you do. That's when I found the video.

It was posted by a 20-year-old, high school graduate. She'd interned at a Start up, right after graduation, before college, and over a year and eight months, she saved a little above $8,500. She filmed herself talking through the journey and jokingly asked, "guys, got any ideas what to do with my savings?" She mentioned she'd gotten a scholarship into college, so most of her tuition and fees were already covered.

As someone who writes about money, frugal living, and wealth creation, I always end up reading the comments on videos like this, partly to see what people would actually suggest, partly to see how people's minds work around money in general. It was a rabbit hole. Some comments were genuinely smart. A lot were just funny.

First, a real congratulations to her. People underestimate how hard it actually is to save, especially as a young person. She had every reason not to. New clothes, new shoes, every trend Instagram could throw at her, and she wasn't even a high earner. She was just an intern at a relatively unknown Start-up, staying disciplined, and stacking money slowly over almost two years. That's genuinely impressive.

So, the real question: what should she actually do with the money?

If she asked me directly, here's exactly how I'd break down $8,000. And if you're a young person reading this with a similar amount saved, this applies to you too.

## 1. Pay yourself 10% first

You're probably expecting me to jump straight into stocks, bonds, or some other investment vehicle. But the first move isn't investing at all. It's treating yourself.

Saving diligently for almost two years takes real discipline. You are your biggest asset, and that deserves acknowledgment. Go do a little shopping. Get a treat. Take yourself out. I'd advocate for roughly 10% of the total going toward this, because if you skip it entirely, part of your brain quietly registers the win as uncelebrated, and that has a real psychological cost down the line.

What the treat actually is depends entirely on you. A movie. A short local trip. Some new dresses. Some gadgets. A spa day, or a meal out with people you love. The only rule here is to optimize for yourself, not for what looks good to other people. Ask what genuinely feels like a reward to you, then go do that.

Ten percent of $8,000 is $800. That leaves $7,200.

## 2. Put 20% into a low-risk asset

Next, take $1,440 (20% of the $7,200 remaining) and put it somewhere genuinely low-risk.

Why low-risk specifically? Because this pocket needs to be accessible fast. If an emergency hits, you need to reach this money without losing value on the way out.

This isn't the stock market, real estate, or anything with a price that moves day to day. Say you put $1,440 into a stock instead, and the market dips right when you need the cash. You're not getting $1,440 back. You're getting whatever the market happens to say it's worth that day, which could be meaningfully less.

The right home for this money is an emergency fund: safety first, a little interest, not dividends, and full liquidity, meaning you can pull it out within 24 hours without penalty. In the US and UK, a high-yield savings account or money market account both fit this exactly. The price shouldn't move. You should always know precisely what's there.

## 3. Put 35% into an ETF

Now take roughly $2,000 (35% of the $5,760 remaining) and put it into an ETF, an Exchange Traded Fund.

Think of it like a fruit smoothie. Instead of buying just an apple, you're buying a blend, apple, orange, pineapple, watermelon, grape, all combined into one drink. Once they're blended, you can't pull the pineapple back out on its own. That's essentially what an ETF is: many different stocks or bonds bundled into a single, tradeable product.

This is often recommended as a lower-risk way to invest for the long run, because it spreads your exposure. If some stocks in the mix are struggling while others are thriving, an ETF lets you benefit from the ones doing well without betting everything on a single company.

One well-known example is the S&P 500 ETF, which tracks the 500 largest publicly traded companies in the US. Investing in it means you own a small slice of all 500 at once. Do your own research on which specific ETF fits your goals, and consider talking to a licensed financial professional before committing real money here.

## 4. Put 15% into a high-risk asset

Fifteen percent of what's left ($3,760), about $564, can go toward something genuinely higher risk, established cryptocurrency, for example.

Yes, crypto is volatile. That's not in dispute. But context matters here. This is a 20-year-old with roughly 45 years until a typical retirement age. She's not in a rush, and this allocation is a deliberately small slice of the total portfolio, not the majority of it.

That's the actual argument for considering a stake in a large, established coin (not a meme coin) and simply holding long-term. If it underperforms, it's a small, contained loss relative to everything else she's built. If it does well, it adds real upside to the portfolio.

The mistake a lot of people make with high-risk assets is going too heavy, putting 50% or more of their total savings into something this volatile. That's a very different risk profile than 15%, and it's usually people without this young saver's runway making that mistake.

If you're young and reading this, starting early with a small, contained allocation like this is genuinely one of the biggest advantages you have. If you're in your 30s or 40s and think it's too late, it isn't. You start from exactly where you are, with what you actually have, and you may simply need to allocate more to catch up on the timeline.

## 5. Put 15% into health coverage

Fifteen percent of what remains ($3,196), about $480, should go toward securing some form of health coverage.

An unexpected illness or accident can wipe out savings built over years in a matter of weeks. Getting covered isn't optional in my view, it's foundational. Whether it's a small clinic visit, an accident, or something more serious, having coverage means you're not entirely at the mercy of other people's generosity when something goes wrong.

There's a second benefit too. Coverage often means faster access to care in the first place, treatment can start before a financial crisis forces delay.

If you're in the US and want to understand what subsidized or free healthcare options might actually be available to you, [ACA Open Enrollment: Dates, Real Costs, and Free Help You Didn't Know Existed](/blog/aca-open-enrollment-guide) breaks that down in full detail.

## What's left: $2,716

At this point, the essential obligations are covered, so what happens to the rest is genuinely up to you. It can absorb a month of regular expenses, pay up debt, or go straight back into savings if that fits your situation better. Either is a reasonable call once everything above has actually been handled.

Note: I didn't factoring debt repayment until the last money left. She didn't mention that and I believe, if she's in debt, she probably would have paid that first before declaring the remaining as her total savings. 

## The real takeaway here

None of this is really about the exact percentages. It's about the habit underneath them: treating a windfall as something to be intentional with, not something to be reacted to. Most people who come into money, whether it's $500 or $50,000, don't lose it because the amount was too small to matter. They lose it because no plan existed before the money showed up.

You don't need a financial advisor's office or a six-figure salary to build that habit. You need a framework, and the discipline to actually follow it before the next trend, sale, or "opportunity" shows up asking for your attention.

Warren Buffett put it about as simply as it can be put: "Do not save what is left after spending; spend what is left after saving."

That single sentence is the entire structure of this post, just in reverse order of how most people actually live it.

## One more thing worth doing

If you're serious about building real wealth over time, pick up a genuinely good financial literacy book. There are plenty out there, but one I keep coming back to is *The Psychology of Money* by Morgan Housel. The core idea running through it is simple: making, keeping, and growing money starts with how you think about it, not just what you know about it.

Financial behavior is downstream of financial mindset. Look at wealthy people who've lost everything once and rebuilt it. The money didn't come back because they got lucky twice. It came back because the underlying thinking that built it the first time was still intact.

If the mindset isn't right, the money moves in circles no matter how much of it shows up.
