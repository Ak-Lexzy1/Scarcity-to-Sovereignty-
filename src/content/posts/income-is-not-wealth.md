You can be a genius at making money and still be terrible at holding onto it. Those are two separate skill sets, and only one of them gets taught.

At his peak in 1991, MC Hammer was earning $30 million a year. He filed for bankruptcy in 1996 with debts of $13.7 million, after spending on 17 luxury cars, two helicopters, a fleet of racehorses, and a $30 million mansion renovation.

Allen Iverson earned roughly $200 million over his NBA career. By 2012, he'd filed for bankruptcy, driven by gambling losses, three costly divorces, and a defaulted six-figure jewelry payment.

Read that again. The money came. The money went.

Michael Jackson died with debts reportedly between $400 and $500 million. He was debt-servicing a lifestyle bigger than even massive income could sustain.

These weren't people who didn't know how to make money. They knew it very well. And plenty of Americans know exactly how this story goes, even without a single luxury car or racehorse involved.

You start earning $9,000 to $15,000 a month. Next thing, a new car. A nicer apartment or house. Designer clothes. Regular vacations. Family responsibilities stacking on top of it all.

Then income drops for a few months, and suddenly you're broke again. The problem you probably didn't recognize here is that income is not wealth.

An increase in income can make you look rich. A good business can make you look successful. A big bonus can make you feel invincible. But if your lifestyle rises every time your income rises, congratulations, you've successfully become a better-paid version of your old financial problems.

But here's the good news.

You don't need to earn $10,000 to $15,000 before becoming financially intelligent. Start with what you have. If you earn $5,000, learn to manage $5,000, and if you earn $15,000, learn to manage $15,000. The habits you build with $5,000 will follow you when $15,000 arrives.

## Think of your money in 4 buckets, which I tagged "SSBR"

**Sustain.** This covers your actual expenses and responsibilities, rent or mortgage, groceries, utilities, the bills that keep your life running.

**Shield.** Your emergency fund and insurance sit here. This bucket exists so a bad month doesn't turn into a bad year.

**Build.** Investments and assets go here, the money that's working for you instead of just sitting there.

**Reward.** Guilt-free spending, on purpose. The fun money, spent without a second thought, because you already planned for it.

The exact percentages in these buckets will differ depending on your situation, but the principle is simple. Every dollar should have a job.

And please, stop thinking investing means putting every dollar you have into stocks because you "want to build wealth." Your first assignment is to build financial stability, then invest consistently, and finally diversify. Understand what you actually own. Avoid lifestyle inflation. And most importantly, don't use tomorrow's money meant to secure you to finance today's appearance.

## Your goal isn't to look wealthy

While looking wealthy isn't a bad thing, it should rather be a complement after you have built the actual wealth, because your goal is to become financially difficult to destroy.

The real flex is this: you can lose your job and survive. Your business can have a bad quarter and you're okay. An emergency doesn't send you borrowing. Your investments keep compounding quietly, and nobody on Instagram or TikTok needs to know.

Making money is a skill. Keeping money is a skill. Growing money is a skill. Protecting money is a skill. All four can be learned.

So if you're currently earning little, don't be ashamed. You're not behind. Start building the financial habits today that your future income will thank you for.
