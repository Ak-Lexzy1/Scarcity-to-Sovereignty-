It's the Monday before Thanksgiving. Your calendar says the office is closed Thursday, your group chat is planning a potluck, and one quiet question sits at the back of your mind: will that day show up on my paycheck?

Most people assume a holiday this big comes with pay. Sometimes it does. But the reason has very little to do with the law and a lot to do with how you're classified and what your employer put in writing. In this post, you'll learn what federal law says about holiday pay, how hourly and salaried pay work differently, and what to check in your handbook before November arrives.

## Is Thanksgiving a Federal Paid Holiday?

Thanksgiving is a federal holiday, but that label is less powerful than it sounds. It's the fourth Thursday of November, and this year it lands on November 26. Federal law (5 U.S.C. 6103) gives most federal employees paid time off on that day. The U.S. Office of Personnel Management says most federal employees get their regular pay when excused from duty on a designated holiday, and extra holiday pay when required to work.

Private employers play by different rules. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), the main federal wage law, doesn't require private employers to pay for holidays or to pay extra if you work them. It only requires pay for time you actually work. You can read the Department of Labor's explanation at [https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/workhours/holidays](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/workhours/holidays).

So why does it feel automatic? Because most employers choose to offer it. Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows 81% of private industry workers had access to paid holidays in 2025, averaging 8 days a year. Surveys of employer benefits typically list Thanksgiving among the near-universal paid days.

**The honest takeaway:** If you get paid on Thanksgiving, it's because of a policy, an offer letter or a contract. It isn't because the calendar says so.

## Do Companies Have to Pay You If You Don't Work on Thanksgiving?

For private-sector workers, the general rule is no, unless your employer promised it. Employment in most states is at-will, which means your employer sets many terms, and your handbook, offer letter or union contract usually defines what you're owed.

Look for these details in the holiday section of your handbook:

- **The list of paid holidays:** Thanksgiving is often on it, but check the wording.
- **Who's eligible:** Some policies cover only full-time staff, or require a waiting period after hire.
- **The attendance rule:** Many employers require you to work your scheduled shifts the day before and the day after the holiday. Miss one without approval, and you can lose the holiday pay. The federal government uses a similar idea: federal employees generally need to be in a pay status before or after the holiday.
- **How the day is paid:** Some policies pay a flat 8 hours. Others pay your scheduled hours.

If your handbook promises paid holidays and your employer doesn't pay them, that's no longer just a "company choice." It may be a broken promise worth raising.

## Do You Get Paid If You Work on Thanksgiving?

Yes, you're paid for the hours you work. Whether you get extra is a different question.

**The myth:** Many people believe time and a half is the law on holidays.

**The reality:** No federal law requires it for private employers. Many do it anyway to attract people to holiday shifts, but it's a perk, not a rule.

Overtime is separate. Federal overtime kicks in after 40 hours in a workweek, not because the day is a holiday. And paid holiday time you didn't work generally doesn't count toward those 40 hours. So a 4-day workweek plus a paid Thanksgiving can leave you short of overtime, even if it feels like a 40-hour week.

### A paycheck example

These numbers are illustrative, not from a real employer. Say you earn $22 an hour and normally work 8-hour days, Monday through Friday.

| Situation | What you'd earn that week |
| --- | --- |
| Company closed Thursday, no holiday pay | 32 hours x $22 = $704 |
| Company closed Thursday, pays 8 holiday hours | 40 hours x $22 = $880 |
| You work 8 hours Thursday at time and a half | $704 + (8 x $33) = $968 |

The gap between the first two rows is $176. That's why knowing your policy before November matters.

## Hourly vs Salaried: How Thanksgiving Pay Works Differently

The biggest factor in your pay isn't the holiday. It's whether you're classified as non-exempt (usually hourly) or exempt (usually salaried). Check your offer letter or pay stub if you're not sure.

### For Hourly, Non-Exempt Workers

- **If the company closes and doesn't offer holiday pay:** You're generally not paid for the day, unless your employer lets you use PTO.
- **If the company offers paid holidays:** You're paid according to the policy, often a standard 8 hours.
- **If you work the holiday:** You're paid for hours worked, and for premium pay if your employer or state requires it.

### For Salaried, Exempt Workers

Salaried workers have stronger protection, and it surprises people. Under the Department of Labor's salary-basis rule (29 CFR 541.602), an exempt employee must get their full salary for any week in which they do any work. Your employer generally can't dock your pay for a day it chooses to close.

Here's how that plays out:

- **Company closes on Thursday only:** You should receive your full weekly salary, even if you have no PTO left.
- **Employer requires PTO use:** An employer can direct you to use accrued PTO for the closure, as long as you still receive your full salary. Department of Labor opinion letters support this. But if you have no PTO, the employer still has to pay your full salary for a closure shorter than a full workweek.
- **Full-week closure:** If the business shuts for an entire week and you do absolutely no work, your employer may not owe your salary for that week. But if you check even a few work emails, you can trigger a full week's pay.
- **You choose to take a day off while the office is open:** A full-day deduction for a personal absence can be allowed under the rule's exceptions.

**A heads-up:** A "paid holiday" and a "required salary payment" are two different things. A salaried worker's Thanksgiving pay may come from the salary-basis rule, not from a holiday benefit.

## What About PTO, Vacation and Sick Time?

**Can your employer require you to use PTO for a closure?** Generally yes, if it applies the rule consistently and has a clear policy. Many employers put this in writing. For salaried workers, it only works if they still receive their full salary.

**Can they force an unpaid day if you have no PTO?**

- **Hourly workers:** Often yes, since they're only owed pay for time worked, unless your policy or contract says otherwise.
- **Salaried workers:** Usually no, for closures shorter than a full workweek.

**What happens to your unused PTO if you leave?** That's a state question. California and Colorado treat earned vacation as wages and don't allow "use it or lose it" rules. Massachusetts also requires payout in most sources, and other states follow whatever your written policy says. Many have no rule. Sources disagree on the exact list, so check your state labor department.

## State Laws and Company Policies That Change Everything

Here's where I need to correct a common assumption. People often point to California and Massachusetts as having tougher holiday-pay rules. When I checked, that's not quite right.

- **Rhode Island** is the standout. Employers must generally pay time and a half for work on state legal holidays, including Thanksgiving, with exceptions for some groups like supervisory staff and hotel and restaurant workers.
- **Massachusetts** restricts most retail businesses from opening on Thanksgiving without a permit. Its old premium-pay rule for retail holiday work was phased out as of January 2023, so premium pay isn't required unless your contract says so.
- **Maine** also restricts many large retailers from operating on Thanksgiving, with exceptions.
- **California** doesn't require holiday pay for hourly workers, but it matters for vacation payout and for salaried workers.

Outside these states, holiday pay is typically a company choice. Cities can also have their own rules, so check locally.

Two more things override the default:

- **Union contracts and written employment contracts** often guarantee paid holidays and premium pay.
- **Your offer letter** may include terms that aren't in the handbook.

**The 2-minute handbook check:** Search your handbook PDF for "holiday," then "eligibility," then "premium." If you can't find it, ask HR to send you the holiday policy in writing.

## How to Find Out If You Get Paid for Thanksgiving at Your Job

1. **Check the handbook under "Holidays" or "Paid time off."** Note the list of holidays and any waiting period.
2. **Ask HR about the eligibility window.** Do you need to work the day before and the day after?
3. **Confirm your classification.** Look at your offer letter or pay stub. Hourly usually means non-exempt. A fixed salary may mean exempt, but job duties also matter.
4. **Look at last year's pay stub.** Check the Thanksgiving week. Was there a holiday line item, or did your hours drop?
5. **Ask about the day after.** Black Friday isn't a federal holiday. Some employers give it as a paid day, but plenty don't.
6. **Ask about Thursday work.** If you may be scheduled to work, ask now whether there's a premium.

## What to Do If You Don't Get Paid and You Think You Should

1. **Document everything:** Save the handbook page, offer letter, schedule and pay stubs. Write down the hours you worked and any messages from your manager.
2. **Talk to HR or your manager first:** Many cases are payroll mistakes. Put your question in writing.
3. **Know which law applies:** If a salaried worker's pay was cut, the Department of Labor's salary-basis rule may apply. If your employer simply broke its own holiday-pay promise, that's often a state wage claim rather than a federal one.
4. **File a wage claim if needed:** The Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division takes complaints at 1-866-487-9243 or at [https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd). Staff can also point you to your state labor agency. Don't wait, because time limits apply. Retaliation for filing is illegal.

## Thanksgiving Pay FAQs

**Do you get paid time and a half on Thanksgiving?**

Not by federal law. Many employers pay it voluntarily, and Rhode Island generally requires it for most employees.

**Can my employer not pay me on Thanksgiving?**

If you're hourly and didn't work, yes, unless a policy or contract promises pay. Salaried workers usually must receive their full salary for a short closure.

**Do part-time workers get holiday pay on Thanksgiving?**

Only if the employer's policy includes them. Some prorate pay and some limit it to full-time staff. Part-time federal employees are paid for holiday hours in their scheduled tour of duty.

**Is Thanksgiving double pay?**

No law requires double pay in private jobs. Some employers offer it for holiday shifts, but it's rare, so check your policy.

## Bottom Line

No federal law makes your boss pay you for Thanksgiving. Most full-time 9-5 jobs pay anyway because of policy, not because the calendar says so. That's why a five-minute look at your handbook now is worth more than a guess in November.
