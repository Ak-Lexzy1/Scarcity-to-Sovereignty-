I must have been around eight years old when a small occurrence made me start paying more attention to birds afterwards. It was a sunny weekend, and I heard this strange, frantic fluttering sound near our kitchen window. I remember dropping whatever I was doing to go check it out.

Two birds were locked in what looked like a fight, wings flapping, while a third bird stood a little distance away, perched on the ground. Every so often it would glance over at the two fighters, almost like it was keeping score.

I can't remember their species, but it was a fascinating sight, and I was completely absorbed in it. So absorbed, in fact, that I didn't even notice my dad had walked up quietly behind me to see what had stolen my attention so completely. This was the first time I had ever seen an up-close brawl of birds. Chickens were the usual ones I was familiar with, from my grandparents' farmhouse.

Eventually, the two fighting birds took the whole thing up to the roof of our shed, still going at it. The third bird eventually just flew off, apparently unimpressed by either contestant. My dad, who happened to be home that particular weekend, explained that the two fighters were most likely males, and the third bird watching from the ground was probably the female they were both trying to impress.

That one explanation turned into an entire afternoon of bird talk. I asked question after question, and he had all the time in the world to answer them, sharing stories and things he'd picked up over the years. My younger sibling later joined our discussion to listen to bird tales from my dad. It's one of those small, ordinary memories that somehow never faded.

Years later, I read about a certain bird that made that whole afternoon come rushing back. You've probably heard of the lyrebird.

This genius isn't your average bird because it can mimic almost anything: other birds, camera shutters, chainsaws, and other mechanical sounds it picks up from its environment. It's a genuinely strange creature, and it might just be one of the best marketing teachers you'll ever come across.

But it's not the mimicry itself that's the real lesson. It's what the lyrebird does with it, similar to the bird fight I witnessed that summer afternoon many years ago.

When it comes to mating, most bird species would engage in a brawl, but the lyrebird has a trick it uses, and this is where it gets interesting. Follow me.

When a female lyrebird tries to leave a male before mating, or during mating itself, he does something remarkable. He mimics the sound of a mobbing flock, which is the panicked, layered alarm calls birds make when a predator is nearby.

It's not real, and there's no predator. It's just the lyrebird, alone, recreating the sound of an entire group in crisis. And it works. The female stays close, because leaving suddenly feels riskier than staying.

This right here is the lesson I want to teach you to apply in your business and personal brand. It's called "the art of positioning."

That lyrebird's trick is not brute manipulation. That's positioning, and it's the reason the male lyrebird doesn't shout louder to keep his mate there. He doesn't even perform harder or fight another male to get her attention. He simply creates a compelling reason to make her stay exactly where she already is.

This is exactly how great marketing works. The best marketers don't shout about how great they are. They give people a reason to stay engaged, right when they're about to click away. It's marketing that resonates at its finest.

This is the hidden psychology behind magnetic marketing. It's not about being the loudest voice in the room. It's about making leaving feel like the wrong move. Being loud is not necessarily bad in marketing. The question you should rather ask yourself is, "Am I making enough sense with all this noise to compel people to pay attention and buy?"

People shouldn't look at your brand and feel intimidated. They should rather feel understood. Your focus should be to create understanding, not intimidation. Talk to their pain points directly. Mirror their emotions back to them. Describe their fears clearly enough that they think, "this person gets me."

When you get this right, your message mirrors their minds.

When your message mirrors what someone's already thinking and feeling, they assume you have the solution before you've even pitched them one. That's the shortcut to trust. That's what makes a brand magnetic instead of just loud.

You've got to be the lyrebird.

If you want to win at marketing, stop shouting. Start mirroring. Give your audience a real reason to stay exactly where they are, instead of scrolling past you.

The secret to powerful marketing isn't shouting louder. It's resonating deeper. Why? Because people don't buy from the best brand. They buy from the one that understands them best.

Start mirroring your audience's emotions, desires, and struggles, and watch attraction turn into conversion and tons of sales.
